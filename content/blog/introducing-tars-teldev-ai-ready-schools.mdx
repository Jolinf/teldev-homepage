AI is changing the way we learn.

A student who does not understand a topic no longer has to wait until the next lesson, the next day, or the next time they can find someone to explain it. They can ask a question, get an explanation, ask again when it still does not make sense, request an example, take a quiz, and keep going until the idea finally clicks.

That changes something fundamental about education. It also raises an important question for schools:

**How do we make sure students use AI to learn, rather than use AI to avoid learning?**

That question led to the **TELDEV AI-Ready Schools Initiative**, or **the TARS initiative**. Our aim is to improve how Nigerian schools understand and use technology. We are starting with AI, and we are starting in Lagos.

## Why the TARS initiative?

AI is already in students' hands. For many of them, ChatGPT and other AI assistants are becoming part of how they search for information, complete assignments and study. Pretending the technology does not exist, or simply banning it, does not remove it from a student's life. It only removes it from the classroom.

We understand why teachers are concerned. AI can produce answers students did not arrive at themselves. It makes it easier to submit work without understanding the subject. It sometimes gives information that is wrong or misleading. And it makes the usual ways of checking what a student actually knows harder to rely on. The tools sold to catch AI use do not solve this either: they are not reliable enough to accuse a student on their own, and they are more likely to wrongly flag students who write in English as a second language.

These concerns are real. But there is another side to the conversation. The same technology that can help a student avoid thinking can also help that student **think better**. The difference is not whether a student uses AI. It is **how they use it**.

The TARS initiative is built around that difference. We believe Nigerian schools should approach AI with understanding, not just fear or excitement. Students need to know how to use it responsibly as a learning tool. Teachers need to understand both what it can do and where it falls short. And schools need an approach to AI that protects the integrity of education while making the most of what the technology offers.

## Every student learns differently

A classroom may hold thirty, forty or more students. The teacher has a syllabus to cover, a timetable to follow and limited time for each topic. Naturally, teaching follows a structure: the teacher explains a concept, students listen, examples are given, questions are answered, and the class moves on.

That is not because teachers do not care about individual students. It is because a classroom needs a method that works for the group.

But students do not all learn at the same pace. One understands a mathematical concept after one explanation. Another needs three examples. Another needs to see it visually, and another understands it best when it is connected to something they already know. A good teacher tries to meet these differences, but there are practical limits to how much one person can personalise a lesson for every student in the room.

**AI can help fill that gap.**

## Learning at your own pace

Imagine a student studying at home. They read their notes but do not understand something. They could leave it and move on, or wait until the next lesson to ask the teacher. Or they could ask an AI assistant:

*"Explain this to me as if I am a beginner."*

If that does not work: *"Give me a real-life example."* Still confused? *"Explain it another way."* Then: *"Quiz me on it."* And after answering: *"Why was my answer wrong?"*

There is no bell to say the lesson is over, no timetable saying the class has moved on, and no limit to how many times they can ask.

This does not replace the teacher. It gives the student **another layer of support** outside the classroom.

## The question a student is afraid to ask

There is another problem that is harder to measure. Some students have questions they simply do not ask. They may be shy, afraid their classmates will laugh, worried the teacher will think they were not paying attention, or convinced the question is too simple. So they stay quiet, and leave the classroom without the one answer that might have made the whole topic make sense.

With an AI learning assistant, there is no embarrassment. A student can ask the question they were afraid to ask, then another, and another. They can ask for a simpler explanation, an analogy, or examples from football, music, business, gaming or everyday life, and keep asking until they understand.

**That freedom to ask is one of the most powerful things AI brings to education.**

## AI does not mean learning without teachers

This is where the conversation needs balance. The TARS initiative is not about replacing teachers with AI.

A teacher brings what AI cannot fully replicate: human judgement, experience, encouragement, context, and an understanding of the students in front of them. The goal is not to remove the teacher from learning. It is to give both teachers and students better tools.

Teachers can use AI to prepare lessons, create practice questions, develop alternative explanations and cut down the repetitive work that takes time away from teaching. Students can use AI to practise, explore, ask questions and reinforce what they have learned.

But students must also learn that AI is not always right. It can give a wrong answer with complete confidence. Students need to learn to question its responses, check them against trusted sources such as their textbook, and use their own judgement. That is an important part of becoming **AI-ready**.

## From AI users to AI-ready students

Knowing how to open an AI tool is not AI literacy. An AI-ready student understands what AI can and cannot do, when it is appropriate to use it, how to ask better questions, how to verify its answers, and where the line falls between getting help and academic dishonesty.

That is the relationship with technology we want students to build. Not *"AI will do my work for me,"* but *"AI can help me understand, practise and explore."*

## What the TARS initiative does

The TARS initiative brings practical AI education into secondary schools, beginning with SS1 and SS2 students, in the years before WAEC, NECO and JAMB when good study habits matter most, and their teachers.

Our student workshops treat AI as a learning companion. In small groups, students work with topics from their own syllabus and learn how AI can help them:

- understand difficult concepts in different ways
- learn at their own pace
- ask questions without fear or embarrassment
- practise with quizzes and exam-style questions
- explore topics beyond the first classroom explanation
- check and challenge what AI tells them
- recognise the difference between using AI to learn and using it to avoid learning

They leave with a prompt card of questions to ask AI when they study on their own.

Teachers are part of the initiative too, because becoming AI-ready is not only a student responsibility. **The whole school needs to understand the technology.** Teachers learn how AI can support lesson preparation, practice materials and marking guides. They also work through practical ways to prevent and respond to misuse and keep assessment fair: designing assignments AI cannot simply complete, using short oral checks, and setting a clear classroom policy on when AI is allowed and when it is not.

AI is where we start. Later sessions will cover other ways technology can support teaching and learning.

## What comes next: SS3

We are also planning a track for SS3 students after their final exams. Many spend months waiting for admission. The SS3 track will focus on practical digital skills they can use to earn, work and keep learning in that time. It is timed after WAEC and NECO so it never competes with exam preparation, and we will share details here when it is ready.

## Building schools that are ready for what comes next

AI is not going away. The question for schools is no longer whether students will encounter it. They will. The question is whether they will encounter it with the knowledge and judgement to use it well.

At TELDEV, we believe technology should not simply be something students are told to use or avoid. They should understand it well enough to make informed decisions about it.

That is the purpose of the TARS initiative: to help schools move from **AI awareness to AI readiness**, giving students the freedom to learn in ways that work for them, and giving teachers the knowledge and tools to guide that learning responsibly.

The future of education should not be about choosing between teachers and technology. **It should be about giving teachers and students the right technology to make learning better.**

## How a school can take part

TELDEV provides the facilitators and workshop materials. For schools that do not have their own, we can also provide devices and internet access, so students never need personal devices or AI accounts to take part. We follow each school's rules on safeguarding and photos.

We are beginning with schools in Lagos and will expand as more schools come on board. Outreach is under way, and we will post updates here as the first workshops take place.
